# Week 15 — Capstone Deploy and Present: Multi-Stage Dockerfiles for ASP.NET Core and Native AOT, a GitHub Actions Build/Test/Publish/Deploy Pipeline, Azure Container Apps on the Free Tier, the RUNBOOK, and the Recorded Demo

Welcome to **C9 · Crunch Sharp**, Week 15 — the last week, and the one where the Polyglot Workshop stops being a thing that runs on your laptop and becomes a thing that runs on a URL a stranger can open. Week 13 made the three clients compile against one `.proto` contract with integration tests green in CI. Week 14 hardened that build — auth covered by tests, logs/metrics/traces flowing to Grafana + Loki + Tempo, MediatR and AutoMapper used where they earned their keep, the OWASP API Top 10 walked at the boundary. This week we take that hardened build and **ship it**, and we ship it the only way that counts: as an artifact a pipeline produced, not a thing you `dotnet run` on your machine and pray about.

The week's thesis is the one in the syllabus and it is worth saying flatly: **deploy is a feature.** The pipeline is part of the product. A service that only its author can deploy, from a laptop, by remembering the right `dotnet publish` flags and the right `az` incantation, is not finished — it is a liability with a demo. The deliverable of this week is a system where a single `git push` to `main` runs the tests, builds a container, publishes it to a registry, and rolls it out to Azure Container Apps, and where the `RUNBOOK.md` in the repo tells a tired on-call engineer at 2am exactly how to roll it back, where the logs live, how to rotate the OIDC client secret, and what to do when the database fills up. The demo is the proof; the runbook is the product.

The first thing to internalize is that **the container is the unit of deploy, and the Dockerfile is where you decide what is in it.** A .NET app does not deploy as "the source on a server" anymore than a Go app does; it deploys as an OCI image, and that image is built once, in CI, from a multi-stage Dockerfile that compiles in a fat SDK stage and copies the published output into a thin runtime stage. The runtime image carries the .NET runtime (or, for Native AOT, nothing but the OS and your self-contained binary), the published DLLs, and nothing else — no SDK, no source, no `.git`, no `node_modules`. The size difference is not cosmetic: a `mcr.microsoft.com/dotnet/sdk:9.0` image is roughly 800 MB; `mcr.microsoft.com/dotnet/aspnet:9.0-noble-chiseled` is roughly 110 MB; a Native AOT binary on `mcr.microsoft.com/dotnet/runtime-deps:9.0-noble-chiseled` is roughly 30 MB plus your binary. Smaller images pull faster, start faster on a scale-from-zero free tier, and present a smaller attack surface. Lecture 1 builds all three.

The second thing to internalize is the **build/test/publish/deploy split in the pipeline**, and the fact that these are four distinct concerns with four distinct failure modes. *Build* compiles and fails on a warning if you treat warnings as errors (you do). *Test* runs xUnit unit tests and the Testcontainers integration tests against ephemeral PostgreSQL and Keycloak, and fails the whole pipeline if one assertion is red — a build that does not pass its tests does not get to become an image. *Publish* produces the container image and pushes it to a registry (GitHub Container Registry, `ghcr.io`, free for public repos) tagged with the commit SHA, so every image is traceable to exactly one commit. *Deploy* takes that immutable, SHA-tagged image and tells Azure Container Apps to run a new revision of it, shifting traffic to the new revision only when it passes its health probe. The discipline that makes this safe is **immutability**: you never rebuild an image to deploy it again; you deploy the exact bytes that passed the tests. Lecture 2 writes the whole `.github/workflows/deploy.yml`.

The third thing to internalize is **Azure Container Apps as the primary target, and why the free tier is enough for a capstone.** Azure Container Apps (ACA) is a serverless container host built on Kubernetes and KEDA that you never have to see the Kubernetes of. It scales to zero when idle (free), scales out under load, terminates TLS for you, gives you a public HTTPS URL, and the free grant — the first 180,000 vCPU-seconds and 360,000 GiB-seconds per month, plus the first 2 million requests — comfortably covers a classroom-platform capstone that is idle most of the time and gets demoed for ten minutes. The trade is a cold start: a scaled-to-zero app takes a few seconds to wake on the first request, which is exactly why the small, AOT-friendly image matters. Fly.io and "any Linux box with Docker" are the documented secondary targets for students who cannot make an Azure account work; the pipeline changes by one job, not by a rewrite. Lecture 2 covers the ACA deploy and the Fly.io fallback.

The fourth thing to internalize is that **a runbook is documentation with a job, and the job is to be correct at 2am for someone who is not you.** A runbook is not a README and it is not an architecture document. It is a flat, numbered, copy-pasteable set of procedures for the operations a human will actually have to perform under stress: deploy a new version, roll back to the previous revision, find the logs for a failing request, rotate the leaked OIDC client secret, drain and resize the database when it fills, put the app in maintenance mode. Every procedure is a sequence of exact commands with exact expected output, because at 2am nobody wants prose — they want the command that fixes it and a way to know it worked. Lecture 3 is entirely about writing the runbook, and the homework grades yours against a "could a stranger execute it cold" bar.

The fifth thing to internalize is that **on-call is a practice, not a vibe**, and even a capstone deserves the small version of it. The runbook is the artifact; the practice around it is: every user-facing failure has an alert that fires (Week 14 wired the burn-rate alerts), every alert links to the runbook section that resolves it, every deploy is reversible in one command, and the team agrees on what "down" means before it is down. We are not asking you to carry a pager. We are asking you to build the thing such that *if* you carried a pager, the runbook would let you sleep. Lecture 3 covers the on-call basics — severity levels, the rollback-first instinct, the blameless habit of writing down what happened.

The sixth thing to internalize is the **shape of the deliverable**, because this is a capstone milestone, not a toy. By Sunday: the Polyglot Workshop backend is reachable at a public HTTPS URL on Azure Container Apps (or Fly.io); the Blazor admin is reachable at its own public URL talking gRPC-Web to that backend; the MAUI client is built as a signed Android APK that sideloads onto a real (or emulated) device and signs in via OIDC against the deployed Keycloak; `RUNBOOK.md` is in the repo and a teammate followed it cold to do a deploy and a rollback; and a 5-to-8-minute recorded demo is linked from the portfolio, tracing one lesson from "instructor creates it" through "learner enrolls and submits on mobile" to "it shows up on the admin dashboard." The mini-project this week **is** that milestone, and its README points at `projects/capstone/` as the master capstone guide — this is the final assembly of the system Weeks 13, 14, and 15 all built.

By the end of the week you will be the person on your team who can take a green PR and turn it into a live URL with one push, who can roll it back in one command when the live URL goes red, and who can hand a stranger a runbook that lets them operate a service they have never seen. That is the difference between a developer and an engineer who ships, and it is where C9 ends.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this week, you will be able to:

- **Write** a multi-stage `Dockerfile` for an ASP.NET Core 9 service: an SDK build stage that restores and publishes, and a chiseled `aspnet:9.0-noble-chiseled` runtime stage that carries only the published output and runs as a non-root user. Explain why each stage exists and what is (and is not) in the final layer. Cite the .NET container docs at <https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dotnet/core/docker/build-container>.
- **Write** a second multi-stage `Dockerfile` that publishes a **Native AOT** binary and runs it on `runtime-deps:9.0-noble-chiseled`, producing an image under ~40 MB. Identify what AOT forbids (no runtime codegen, constrained reflection, no `MakeGenericType` on unseen types) and confirm your AOT-published surface respects it. Cite <https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dotnet/core/deploying/native-aot/>.
- **Author** a GitHub Actions workflow with four logical phases — build, test, publish, deploy — where test failure blocks publish and publish produces a SHA-tagged image in `ghcr.io`. Cite the GitHub Actions docs at <https://docs.github.com/en/actions> and the `docker/build-push-action` at <https://github.com/docker/build-push-action>.
- **Run** the integration tests (`WebApplicationFactory<T>` + Testcontainers for PostgreSQL and Keycloak) **inside** the CI runner, understanding why Testcontainers needs the Docker socket and how to make it available on a GitHub-hosted runner. Cite <https://dotnet.testcontainers.org/>.
- **Deploy** a container image to **Azure Container Apps** on the free tier with `az containerapp up` / `az containerapp update`, configure ingress, secrets, environment variables, and a health probe, and obtain a public HTTPS URL. Cite <https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/container-apps/get-started>.
- **Configure** the same deploy against **Fly.io** (`fly launch` / `fly deploy`) as the documented fallback, and articulate which one line of the pipeline changes. Cite <https://fly.io/docs/launch/>.
- **Build** the MAUI client as a signed Android APK (`dotnet publish -f net9.0-android -c Release`), sideload it onto a device or emulator, and complete an OIDC sign-in against the deployed Keycloak. Cite <https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dotnet/maui/android/deployment/publish-cli>.
- **Pin** every deployed image to an immutable tag (the commit SHA), and reason about why deploying `:latest` to production is the single most common and most avoidable cause of "it worked yesterday."
- **Write** a `RUNBOOK.md` with numbered, copy-pasteable procedures for deploy, rollback, log retrieval, OIDC-secret rotation, and database-full recovery, each with exact commands and expected output. Defend it against the "a stranger executes it cold at 2am" bar.
- **Roll back** an Azure Container Apps deployment by reactivating the previous revision (`az containerapp revision activate`) in one command, and explain why revision-based rollback is faster and safer than rebuilding the previous image.
- **Articulate** on-call basics: severity levels, the rollback-first instinct, linking every alert to a runbook section, and the blameless postmortem habit.
- **Record** a 5-to-8-minute demo that traces one lesson end to end across all three clients and the deployed backend, and link it from the C9 portfolio.

## Topics covered

- **Multi-stage Dockerfiles.** The SDK build stage, the chiseled runtime stage, layer caching, `.dockerignore`, the non-root `$APP_UID` user, why you copy `*.csproj` and `dotnet restore` before copying the rest of the source.
- **Chiseled images.** `aspnet:9.0-noble-chiseled` and `runtime-deps:9.0-noble-chiseled` — what "chiseled" removes (shell, package manager, most of the OS), why that is good for security and bad for `docker exec` debugging, and the `-chiseled-extra` variant when you need ICU/TLS roots.
- **Native AOT containers.** Publishing `<PublishAot>true</PublishAot>`, the cross-compile toolchain in the build image, running the self-contained binary on `runtime-deps`, the size win, the startup win, the reflection constraints.
- **GitHub Actions.** Workflow triggers, jobs, `needs:` ordering, the `permissions:` block for `ghcr.io`, OIDC federation to Azure (no long-lived cloud credentials in secrets), `docker/build-push-action`, `azure/login`, caching the .NET restore.
- **CI integration tests.** Running `WebApplicationFactory<T>` + Testcontainers on a GitHub-hosted runner, the Docker-in-the-runner story, why the test job must complete before the publish job starts.
- **The registry.** GitHub Container Registry (`ghcr.io`), package visibility, the SHA tag vs `:latest`, immutability as a deploy discipline.
- **Azure Container Apps.** The environment, the app, ingress (external/internal), secrets, environment variables, the health probe, revisions, traffic splitting, scale-to-zero, the free grant.
- **Revision-based rollback.** `az containerapp revision list`, `revision activate`, `revision deactivate`, traffic weights, and why a previous revision is already-built bytes you can re-point traffic at instantly.
- **Fly.io fallback.** `fly.toml`, `fly launch`, `fly deploy`, `fly secrets set`, the one-job pipeline change.
- **MAUI Android release publish.** The keystore, `dotnet publish -f net9.0-android -c Release`, the signed APK, sideloading via `adb install`, OIDC against the deployed Keycloak from a real device.
- **The runbook.** Structure (one procedure per operation), the deploy/rollback/logs/secret-rotation/database-full procedures, the "exact command + expected output" rule, where it lives in the repo.
- **On-call basics.** Severity levels, the rollback-first instinct, alert-to-runbook linkage, the blameless postmortem, what "done deploying" means.
- **The recorded demo.** What to show, the end-to-end lesson trace across three clients, length discipline, the portfolio link.
- **The capstone milestone.** Live backend URL, live admin URL, sideloadable MAUI APK, `RUNBOOK.md` in the repo, recorded demo in the portfolio — the Week 15 deliverable and the final assembly of the Polyglot Workshop.

## The "build succeeded" promise — extended to "deploy succeeded"

C9 has treated `dotnet build` output as a contract all term:

```
Build succeeded · 0 warnings · 0 errors · 412 ms
```

For Week 15 we extend it to a **deploy contract**: every change you ship this week reaches production the same way — `git push`, the pipeline runs green, a SHA-tagged image lands in the registry, a new revision rolls out, the health probe passes, and the public URL serves the new version. A deploy nobody can reproduce is not a deploy; it is an outage waiting to be triggered by the first person who tries to repeat it. The pipeline is the reproduction. The runbook is the recovery.

We add a **rollback contract** too: **every deployed revision must be reversible in one command**, and you must have run that command at least once, against the real deployment, before the demo. The most expensive moment to discover that your rollback does not work is the moment you need it. Thursday's exercise makes you do a real rollback on purpose, while nothing is on fire.

> **Note on cost.** Everything in this week stays inside free tiers. GitHub Actions is free for public repos. `ghcr.io` is free for public images. Azure Container Apps has a monthly free grant (180,000 vCPU-seconds, 360,000 GiB-seconds, 2M requests) that covers a capstone that is idle most of the time. Fly.io's free allowance covers a small always-on machine. The only thing that can cost money is leaving a non-scale-to-zero workload running for weeks — so the runbook's last procedure is "tear it down when the demo is graded," and you should run it.
